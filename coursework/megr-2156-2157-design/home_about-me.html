About Me

My name is Zechariah (Zac) Diegelman, and I am pursuing a degree in Mechanical Engineering because it brings together everything I enjoy most—creativity, problem-solving, designing, building, and challenging myself. After working a variety of trade and labor-based jobs following high school, I realized that while I enjoyed the hands-on nature of those roles, I wanted something more: a career that would constantly challenge me intellectually while also letting me create and innovate. Mechanical engineering checked every box, combining the practical with the imaginative. I’m especially excited to deepen my understanding of design, prototyping, and the process of turning ideas into real, functional solutions.

As a mechanical engineer, I would describe myself as unconventional. Growing up, school was never my strength—I needed tutoring in math, and my real passions were sports and time with friends. Yet, I always loved tinkering and inventing things. Characters like Iron Man and Batman, who were constantly designing and building, inspired me. One of my earliest projects was in eighth grade when I built a taser from a disposable camera and entered it into the science fair. That experience showed me how much joy I found in creating, even outside of school assignments.

After high school, I worked in many different roles—a server, a farmhand, a carpenter, a landscaper, and even a lead technician. Each job taught me something, but I struggled with direction until I finally asked myself: What am I good at? What do I enjoy? What career allows me to grow and still make a living? The answer was clear: mechanical engineering. Once I committed, everything clicked. With a real goal in front of me, I excelled in school in ways I never expected. My favorite experiences so far have been building a robot from scratch using Arduino in my Intro to Engineering class and redesigning a pullback toy car into a car-plane hybrid using SolidWorks. Those projects reminded me how much I enjoy getting lost in the process of designing, iterating, and problem-solving.

One concept in mechanical engineering design that fascinates me is force. At first, it was a struggle, but it became one of my favorite topics as I worked through statics and physics. Newton’s Second Law, F = ma, lays the foundation by showing how force, mass, and acceleration interact. I still remember the moment I realized that weight is not the same as mass—weight is a force, while mass is simply a scalar value without acceleration. Learning to break problems down into Free Body Diagrams helped me visualize external versus internal forces, and I enjoyed how trigonometry and vector analysis could be used to resolve forces in the x and y directions. What excites me most is how these principles connect to real-world applications: for example, force directly relates to work, since W = F·d. If there’s no displacement, then no work is done—a simple but powerful insight. These interconnected principles show how every mechanical design, from the smallest mechanism to the largest structure, rests on these fundamental ideas.

For me, mechanical engineering is not just a degree—it is a way of channeling creativity, discipline, and curiosity into tangible designs and solutions. My path here may not have been straightforward, but every twist and turn has made me more driven to succeed in this field.

Analysis of Design: Gillette SkinGuard

The main problem that the Gillette SkinGuard shaving razor cartridge solves is to aid in preventing razor bumps, burns, and ingrown hairs. The author of the Gillette SkinGuard Razor Cartridges is patented by Vincent Paul Walker, JR. Walker Jr. has worked or is working at Gillette Co LLC, in Boston, Massachusetts United States. Click HERE for the patent.

Geometric Effects

  1. The razor cartridge contains 2 single blades in between a SkinGuard irritation bar (width ~1.5-4mm) to help aid the razor cartridge from allowing the blades to get too close to the skin which helps prevent micro tears and cuts from shaving. The blades are at an angle which helps with the cutting/trimming of facial hairs. The cartridge attaches to the handle in a way that allows it to pivot side to side, enabling the blade angle to adjust to the curvature of your facial structure. Using a lower blade tangent angle (70 in the patent) may give you a smoother or more comfortable shaving experience. For example, the first blade may be around a 15° angle, with it ranging between 10° and 25°(10).  (Image 1)

   2. The razor cartridge has a housing unit that incorporates a specifically molded out SkinGuard bar. This bar is made up of many different ribs that are parallel to one another and positioned normal to the path of the blades. The ribs act as a protective guard between the skin and the blades by keeping the blades from direct contact, while it also allows water through the parallel openings to help with cleaning, and the shaving process. The first and second blades are designed to be on either side of the SkinGuard bar with this design the guard is actively placing distance between contact to help with  reducing irritation, cuts, and bumps. Click HERE for an animation of the guard in action. (Image 2)

   3. There is also a secondary guard (22) which helps aid in separating the hairs by organizing them before the first blade (18) cuts the hairs. This guard helps with the primary guard (204). (Image 3)

  4. There are 2 separate clips on either side of the razor cartridge to keep everything intact. There is one on the left (34a) and one on the right(34b). (Image 4)

  5. There is a water leachable shaving aid (110) which is another support for a smooth glide, and clean shave. (Image 5)

Different Material

  1. If the razor cartridge was made with more than two razor blades it would make the product more prone to giving people razor burns, cuts, and bumps. The razor cartridge was made with just enough razor blades to make it most effective at preventing razor irritation. This also helps if you need to go over a spot more than once, because in doing so with ordinary razor blade cartridges it can irritate the skin. 
  2. If the guards were made from rigid plastics versus them being made from elastomeric materials, the guards wouldn’t give the same effect that they do. The elastomeric materials used aid in aligning the hairs directly in front of the blades, as well as stretching the skin to give the clean shave. Versus if they were made from rigid plastics, the guards would not be able to stretch and align with the hairs as well.

Manufactured

The Gillette SkinGuard shaving razor cartridges are manufactured in both China and Germany by plastic injection molding, followed by the attachment of the blades and lubrication strip.
