Topic: Likelihood of Failure Modes in Truss Components

There are many different likelihoods for Truss components to fail. One of them being a bending moment influence on truss girders. This is caused by rotational stiffness of the joints, however they mainly depend on absolute and relative member stiffness, as well as the basic magnitude of the stress in a truss for example, like a static system. This type of instability can cause a truss’ member at the joint to bend due to a moment/couple force that is being applied at the joint between the member and joint connection. This can cause the joint and even the joint to fail completely.
    Another way a truss can fail is by a fracture-critical truss. This is probably one of the most detrimental truss failures because when one component of the truss fails, it brings the entire truss as a whole down with it. This is one of the biggest dangers when it comes to simple trusses. This happens because there is no alternative load path for a particular force that would usually be supported by another member. 
    Specifically geared toward lightweight steel trusses, they can be extremely dangerous due to the fact that they can become weakened by heat. Since they are usually made pretty thin, the trusses can have very little heat/fire resistance. 
    When it comes to truss failures, it looks like the most important one to consider is known as a phenomenon called progressive collapse. In this type of failure it is almost identical to the “fracture-critical truss” where an initial failure of a specifically structural steel truss can trigger a chain reaction of failures geared toward other components in a truss. This can happen even in a very short period of time, and usually takes place rather quickly. 
    Trusses depending on the material can face a plastic deformation which will fracture the truss, however steel usually stays in the elastic deformation region which allows it to be more ductile. 

Part 1 – Truss Members

The member CG would definitely be the first one to give out. My entire truss is made from A500 steel, and this steel is considered ductile, under a load it will more than likely undergo yielding or elastic deformation before a fracture. It is the member that faces the most force on my entire truss. This member is in compression. It would start to buckle, but in my opinion if the material has a low tensile yield ultimate strength it would definitely fracture. . After buckling all the load would then rely on member CF. Member CF only has an internal force of 5kN, so a sudden force of 16.67kN would definitely be a shock. I believe a buckle like that could cause a fracture-critical truss which would cause the truss as a whole to fail, each component. Stress = 16.67kN/185mm^2 =90.12MPa. A design modification would be to add more supports along its normal to the surface connecting to other members to help with some of the load, but also to prevent the buckling from happening. 
For member CF on my truss, this member would experience a compressive force. Since this member is directly below the external applied force of 15kN it would more than likely buckle. This member would feel a force of 5kn, while this doesn't seem like much, over time it may begin to creep. Also since it is made from lightweight steel it could cause it to weaken which would put even more stress and force back on member CG. Again, starting that chain reaction. Stress = 5kN/185mm^2 = 27.03MPa. A design modification would be to have other members along its vertical for support, preferable in the middle of the member. 
When it comes to a tensile force causing a failure, that would more than likely happen first on member DG which would cause a yielding failure. That member is holding the most internal tensile force of 10kN. If the steel is too lightweight and also gets too hot, it could even start necking. Stress = 10kN/185mm^2 = 54.05MPa. A design modification could be to add on more members to help hold up the tensile force. 
Member AD is also holding close to the same amount of internal force as member DG. While this one is more than likely not going to be the first member to fail, it would be a yielding failure from all the tension that would do it. Stress = 8.34kN/185mm^2 = 45.08MPa.There could be added members to the same pin connections to assist with the force. 
Member BC would fail due to a buckling failure. There is no support in the middle to help keep it from bending out of place. Stress = 6.67kN/185mm^2 = 36.05MPa. Some added members normal to its surface at the middle would help prevent the bending movement. 
Member BF is under a constant tensile force. That force would cause a yielding failure and put more stress on the member FG. Stress = 8.34kN/185mm^2 = 45.08MPa.There could be another support between the BF member and the CF member to take some of the load. 
FG has got quite a few (3 force members) members connected to it. This member is in tension, it would most likely feel that tensional force pulling it apart. It would fail from a yielding failure. Stress = 6.67kN/185mm^2 = 36.05MPa. We could have an additional member, two to be exact at pin F and G, this would help distribute the load. 
The last member AG is in a constant state of compression. If this one were to fail it would be by buckling as well. This member is very important because it is one of the members that holds everything together at pin A. Stress = 6.67kN/185mm^2 = 36.05MPa.We could put a member across A to the middle of DG to help with the load.

Part 2 – Pin Connections

When it comes to the pins, the expected failure mode would be a shear stress failure. Since there are forces acting on either side of the pin, in opposite directions it would cause the shear stress to tear or at least deform the pin. If strong enough it could even cause it to fracture completely. This would be catastrophic if the truss were supporting a bridge, since it would collapse like a domino effect. One after another, since not only one member but two would slip out of place causing them to all fail, resulting in the detrimental failure and collapse. As FASTENAL states fatigue failures have very little plastic deformation or necking, this causes them to fracture completely. A design modification to help prevent the shear stress failure on my truss would be to implement a double shear connection. The next step if that fails would be to have more than one pin connection at a multi member connection to ease the load and make it more uniform across many pins.
